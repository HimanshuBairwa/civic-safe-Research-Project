\begin{abstract}
Crime records are not crime. Police records are produced by a loop: past records
direct patrols, patrols produce records, and the loop amplifies whatever
disparity it started with. A forecaster fit on such records can be well
calibrated against the record and badly wrong about reality, and no amount of
held-out validation on records will reveal it. We build CIVIC-SAFE, a
spatiotemporal forecaster that predicts a full zero-inflated negative binomial
distribution of weekly crime counts per community area, and we pair it with a
correction for this measurement problem. Two contributions. First, the applied
system: a dual-graph GATv2 spatiotemporal transformer with a five-seed
entropy-regularized EMOS ensemble reaches CRPS 2.8267 on Chicago and 3.1401 on
New York for 2023, beating four deep baselines in all eight head-to-head
Diebold--Mariano tests at $p < 2\times10^{-6}$, with conformal intervals achieving
90.75\% and 90.02\% coverage under a pre-registered 3\% disparity ceiling.
Second, and the reason for the paper: we deflate the recorded rate by a feedback
multiplier and issue intervals for the latent process. In simulation at feedback
gain 0.85, intervals calibrated on the record retain only 16.2\% coverage of true
incidence while our corrected intervals hold 93.0\% --- though they abstain on
85\% of cells to do it, a cost we quantify rather than hide. On real records from
both cities, correction cuts exposure disparity by 58\% and 61\%. We report what
fails too: Chicago's probability integral transform is non-uniform at
$p = 5.25 \times 10^{-47}$, and 65 of its 77 areas come in low.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
\IEEEPARstart{S}{tart} with the thing that makes this problem different from
ordinary forecasting. When a weather model predicts rain, the prediction does not
change whether it rains. When a crime model predicts crime, the prediction sends
officers, and officers find crime. Not because they manufacture it, but because
crime that nobody looks for goes unrecorded. Send more patrols to a block and the
recorded rate there rises even if the underlying rate held perfectly still.

So the training data is an artifact of past decisions by the system you are
trying to improve.

This has an uncomfortable consequence. Take a forecaster and validate it the
normal way --- hold out 2023, compute the Continuous Ranked Probability Score
(CRPS), check the coverage of your prediction intervals. Suppose everything
passes. You have learned that the model predicts \emph{records} well. You have
learned nothing about whether it predicts \emph{crime} well, and the two come
apart exactly where it matters most: in the neighbourhoods that were over-policed
to begin with. Ensign \emph{et al.}~\cite{ensign2018runaway} named this loop and
showed it runs away. van Amsterdam \emph{et al.}~\cite{vanamsterdam2025when}
showed the general pattern of a model that is accurate and harmful at once,
invisible to passive validation. What nobody has offered is a fix.

That is what we build here.

Our starting point is a fixed-point model. Latent incidence $\lambda_s$ in area
$s$ is unobserved. A policy allocates attention $a_s = \varphi(\mu_s)$ from the
recorded-rate estimate $\mu_s$. Recording is attention-dependent,
$y_s \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_s\, g(a_s))$ with $g$ increasing, and a
learner that fits the records converges to the self-consistent equilibrium
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fixedpoint}
\mu_s = \lambda_s\, g\!\left(\varphi(\mu_s)\right).
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{sec:theory} develops what follows from \eqref{eq:fixedpoint}: a
feedback gain $\kappa$, an amplification law with a pole at $\kappa = 1$, and the
deflation that inverts it. We want to be careful about credit, because the
amplification form $1/(1-\kappa)$ is classic --- it is the closed-loop gain of a
feedback amplifier, and in economics the social multiplier of Glaeser, Sacerdote
and Scheinkman~\cite{glaeser2003social}. We claim neither. What we contribute is
the constructive step: given an estimate of $\kappa$, deflate the record and
issue prediction intervals valid for $\lambda$ rather than for $y$, abstaining
where the correction stops being trustworthy.

The empirical picture, in brief. CIVIC-SAFE forecasts a full Zero-Inflated
Negative Binomial (ZINB) distribution per area--week--category and reaches CRPS
2.8267 (Chicago) and 3.1401 (NYC) on 2023, ahead of every baseline we tried, with
all eight head-to-head Diebold--Mariano comparisons against deep baselines
significant at $p < 2\times10^{-6}$. Post-hoc conformal calibration lands at 90.75\% and
90.02\% coverage against a 90\% target while holding demographic coverage
disparity at 0.0238 and 0.0286, both under a ceiling fixed at 0.03 before we
looked. Then the correction: at $\kappa = 0.85$ in a closed-loop simulation,
intervals calibrated on the record cover the latent process 16.2\% of the time
while corrected intervals hold 93.0\%. On real Chicago and NYC records,
correction reduces recorded-exposure disparity for the higher-minority stratum by
58\% and 61\%.

We also found things that do not work, and we would rather say them here than
have a referee say them. Chicago's PIT histogram is not uniform
($\chi^2 = 241.84$ on nine degrees of freedom, $p = 5.25 \times 10^{-47}$), so
the ZINB is misspecified in Chicago's right tail; the spatial residuals agree,
with 65 of 77 areas coming in low. A single CIVIC-SAFE seed loses to TFT-ZINB, so
the win rests on ensembling. And the corrected intervals at $\kappa = 0.85$
abstain on 85\% of cells, which means the headline $16\% \to 93\%$ compares two
different denominators. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} collects these.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A feedback-corrected conformal predictor that restores coverage of the
  latent process from a biased record, with principled abstention
  (Section~\ref{sec:latent}). Prior work diagnoses this pathology; to our
  knowledge this is the first construction that corrects it.
  \item A coordinate-free form of the amplification elasticity, with $\kappa$ a
  product of two log-elasticities, and the disparity power law that follows
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:amplification}) --- a sharpening
  of~\cite{ensign2018runaway} that credits~\cite{glaeser2003social} for the gain.
  \item An exactness result for the deflation (Theorem~\ref{thm:deflation}),
  together with the sensitivity bound that motivates the abstention rule and the
  conditional nature of the resulting guarantee.
  \item A competitive applied forecaster with honest calibration: ten conformal
  variants compared under constraint-based selection against a pre-registered
  fairness ceiling, plus a full accounting of failure modes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% =====================================================================
\textbf{Spatiotemporal count forecasting.} ZINB output heads on graph networks
are established for sparse urban counts. STZINB-GNN~\cite{zhuang2022uncertainty}
pairs a zero-inflated negative binomial likelihood with a spatiotemporal graph
encoder, and STMGNN-ZINB~\cite{wang2024spatiotemporal} adds multi-graph fusion.
Our predictor sits in this family and we make no architectural novelty claim for
it. GATv2~\cite{brody2022attentive} supplies the attention operator; we use two
adjacency structures rather than one.

\textbf{Conformal prediction.} Split conformal prediction gives
distribution-free finite-sample coverage under exchangeability. Conformalized
quantile regression~\cite{romano2019conformalized} supplies our score, adaptive
conformal inference~\cite{gibbs2021adaptive} handles drift by updating the
miscoverage target online, and equalized conditional risk
control~\cite{feldman2021improving} gives per-group coverage through
concentration bounds. All are applied here as published. What conformal
prediction guarantees, though, is coverage of the variable you calibrated
against --- the record. That is the gap this paper addresses.

\textbf{Feedback loops in predictive policing.} Ensign
\emph{et al.}~\cite{ensign2018runaway} formalized the runaway loop with
attention-dependent recording and showed a P\'olya-urn model drives allocation to
a degenerate fixed point. We differ in two ways: our amplification is a smooth
power law with a finite pole at $\kappa = 1$ rather than unconditional runaway,
and we supply a correction. Hashimoto
\emph{et al.}~\cite{hashimoto2018fairness} and Wyllie
\emph{et al.}~\cite{wyllie2024fairness} study disparity amplification in
retraining loops empirically, without a closed-form exponent or a fix.

\textbf{Performativity and identification.} That predictions change the world
they predict is the subject of performative
prediction~\cite{perdomo2020performative}, and Perdomo~\cite{perdomo2025revisiting}
shows a predictor can pass every observable calibration check while being
performatively useless. The distinction we need is sharper: here a latent target
$\lambda$ is genuinely different from the recorded $y$, so the failure is a
coverage failure against an unobserved variable. Performative risk
control~\cite{li2025performative} controls risk on \emph{observed} outcomes,
which is the opposite of what is needed. That the pathology is undetectable
passively but identifiable under perturbation is a known
template~\cite{mendler2022anticipating}; we use its difference-in-differences
instantiation rather than claiming the principle.

\textbf{Detection-shock natural experiments.} Staggered acoustic gunshot
detection rollouts have been used as quasi-experiments for recorded crime
outcomes~\cite{connealy2024staggered}. Those studies estimate reduced-form
treatment effects. We reinterpret the same design as a way to estimate a
structural recording elasticity, which is the methodological move we do claim.

\section{Feedback Amplification and Latent Recovery}
\label{sec:theory}
% =====================================================================
This section states what follows from \eqref{eq:fixedpoint}. Throughout,
$M = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s} \mu_s$ denotes the mean recorded rate, and all
elasticities are evaluated at an interior fixed point.

\begin{definition}[Closed-loop feedback gain]
\label{def:kappa}
The feedback gain of the recording loop is the product of the allocation
elasticity and the detection elasticity,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kappa}
\kappa \;=\; \left(\frac{d \log a}{d \log \mu}\right)
             \left(\frac{d \log g}{d \log a}\right),
\end{equation}
that is, how strongly allocation responds to the record times how strongly
recording responds to allocation.
\end{definition}

Writing $\varphi(\mu) = (\mu/M)^{\beta}$ for the allocation policy and
$g(a) = a^{\rho}$ for the recording response makes both elasticities in
\eqref{eq:kappa} constant, with $\beta$ the \emph{policy elasticity} (how
aggressively allocation chases the record) and $\rho$ the \emph{detection
elasticity} (how much extra attention converts into extra recorded incidents).
Then $\kappa = \beta\rho$, and this is the parameterization we simulate.
Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the symbols used throughout.

\begin{theorem}[Feedback amplification]
\label{thm:amplification}
Let $\varphi$ and $g$ be smooth and increasing, and let $\mu$ be an interior fixed
point of \eqref{eq:fixedpoint} at which $\kappa < 1$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] \emph{(Local elasticity.)} The recorded rate responds to the latent
  rate with elasticity
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:elasticity}
  \frac{d \log \mu_s}{d \log \lambda_s} \;=\; \frac{1}{1 - \kappa}.
  \end{equation}
  \item[(ii)] \emph{(Global power law.)} If in addition $\kappa$ does not vary
  with $\mu$ --- as under $\varphi(\mu) = (\mu/M)^{\beta}$ and $g(a) = a^{\rho}$,
  which give $\kappa = \beta\rho$ --- then $\mu \propto \lambda^{1/(1-\kappa)}$,
  so for two groups with true incidence ratio
  $\Delta_{\lambda} = \lambda_1 / \lambda_2$ the recorded ratio is
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:powerlaw}
  \Delta_y \;=\; \left(\Delta_{\lambda}\right)^{1/(1-\kappa)},
  \end{equation}
  a power law with a pole at $\kappa^{*} = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{IEEEproof}
(i) Take logarithms of \eqref{eq:fixedpoint}:
$\log \mu_s = \log \lambda_s + \log g(\varphi(\mu_s))$. Write
$E = d \log \mu_s / d \log \lambda_s$ for the elasticity of interest.
Differentiating with respect to $\log \lambda_s$ and applying the chain rule to
the second term gives
\begin{equation}
E = 1 + \left(\frac{d \log g}{d \log a}\right)
        \left(\frac{d \log a}{d \log \mu}\right) E
  = 1 + \kappa E,
\end{equation}
where the bracketed product is $\kappa$ by Definition~\ref{def:kappa}. Since
$\kappa \neq 1$, solving gives $(1-\kappa)E = 1$, which is
\eqref{eq:elasticity}.
(ii) If $\kappa$ is constant, \eqref{eq:elasticity} is a constant-elasticity
relation whose integral is $\log\mu = \log\lambda/(1-\kappa) + c$. The additive
constant cancels on taking the ratio of two groups, giving \eqref{eq:powerlaw}.
\end{IEEEproof}

Three remarks. The exponent is finite for every $\kappa < 1$, so amplification is
smooth rather than the winner-take-all collapse of~\cite{ensign2018runaway}.

Part (i) holds for any smooth increasing $\varphi, g$ and depends on them only
through the product $\kappa$, which is the sense in which the elasticity is
coordinate-free. Part (ii) is the stronger statement and genuinely needs
$\kappa$ constant: for general $\varphi, g$ the gain varies with $\mu$, and hence
with $\lambda$, so no single global exponent exists. We therefore treat $\kappa$
as a \emph{local} elasticity at the fixed point rather than a universal
constant, and read \eqref{eq:powerlaw} as the constant-gain idealization that our
simulations instantiate.

Stability is immediate in logarithms. Under the power-law parameterization the
fixed-point iteration is
$\log\mu \leftarrow \log\lambda + \kappa(\log\mu - \log M)$ with $M$ the current
mean, so in log coordinates its Jacobian is
$\kappa\left(I - \mathbf{1}w^{\!\top}\right)$ with
$w_j = \mu_j / (S M)$ summing to one. That matrix is $\kappa$ times a rank-one
perturbation of the identity whose eigenvalues are $1$ with multiplicity $S-1$
and $0$, so the spectral radius is exactly $\kappa$ and the iteration contracts
precisely when $\kappa < 1$ --- the same threshold that produces the pole in
\eqref{eq:powerlaw}.

\subsection{Deflation recovers the latent rate exactly}
Under the constant-elasticity parameterization, \eqref{eq:fixedpoint} becomes
$\mu_s = \lambda_s (\mu_s/M)^{\kappa}$. Define the \emph{recording multiplier}
and the deflated rate
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:deflation}
m_s = \left(\frac{\mu_s}{M}\right)^{\kappa},
\qquad
\hat{\lambda}_s = \frac{\mu_s}{m_s} = M^{\kappa}\,\mu_s^{1-\kappa}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact latent recovery and conditional coverage]
\label{thm:deflation}
Let $\mu$ satisfy $\mu_s = \lambda_s(\mu_s/M)^{\kappa}$ with $\kappa < 1$ and
$M$ the mean of the same $\mu$. Then
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] \emph{(Exactness.)} $\hat{\lambda}_s = \lambda_s$ for every $s$ ---
  the deflation \eqref{eq:deflation} is the exact algebraic inverse of the
  recording fixed point, with no unidentified constant.
  \item[(ii)] \emph{(Coverage.)} Consequently, if $y^{\mathrm{lat}}_s \sim
  \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_s)$ and $\kappa$ is known, then writing $Q^{\mathrm{Pois}}_p(\cdot)$
  for the Poisson quantile function, the interval at the deflated rate,
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:latent_interval}
  \widehat{C}_s = \left[\,
    Q^{\mathrm{Pois}}_{\alpha/2}(\hat{\lambda}_s),\;
    Q^{\mathrm{Pois}}_{1-\alpha/2}(\hat{\lambda}_s)
  \,\right],
  \end{equation}
  covers $y^{\mathrm{lat}}_s$ with probability at least $1-\alpha$, the
  inequality being due only to lattice discreteness.
  \item[(iii)] \emph{(Sensitivity.)} Under an estimate $\hat\kappa \ne \kappa$,
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sensitivity}
  \log \hat{\lambda}_s - \log \lambda_s
   = (\kappa - \hat\kappa)\,\log\!\left(\mu_s / M\right),
  \end{equation}
  so on the retained set
  $\mathcal{R} = \{s : \hat\kappa < \kappa_{\mathrm{run}},\;
   m_s \in [\bar{m}^{-1}, \bar{m}]\}$ the log-error is bounded by
  $|\kappa - \hat\kappa|\,(\log \bar{m})/\hat\kappa$.
  \item[(iv)] \emph{(Conditionality.)} $\mathcal{R}$ is determined by $\mu$ alone
  and by no outcome, so applying the same rule to calibration and test cells
  leaves the guarantee valid \emph{conditional on retention}. It says nothing
  about the cells where the system abstains.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{IEEEproof}
(i) The fixed point rearranges directly to
$\lambda_s = \mu_s (\mu_s/M)^{-\kappa}$, which is the definition of
$\hat\lambda_s$ in \eqref{eq:deflation}; the two expressions are identical, so
$\hat\lambda_s = \lambda_s$. Note this uses the \emph{same} normalization $M$ on
both sides, which is what removes the constant that a
$\mu \propto \lambda^{1/(1-\kappa)}$ argument would otherwise leave free.
(ii) is immediate from (i): $\widehat{C}_s$ in \eqref{eq:latent_interval} is
built at the true rate, and Poisson quantile intervals are conservative on a
lattice.
(iii) From \eqref{eq:deflation},
$\log\hat\lambda_s = \log\mu_s - \hat\kappa \log(\mu_s/M)$, while (i) gives
$\log\lambda_s = \log\mu_s - \kappa\log(\mu_s/M)$; subtracting yields
\eqref{eq:sensitivity}. On $\mathcal{R}$, $m_s \in [\bar{m}^{-1},\bar{m}]$ means
$|\hat\kappa \log(\mu_s/M)| \le \log \bar{m}$, hence
$|\log(\mu_s/M)| \le (\log \bar{m})/\hat\kappa$, and the bound follows.
(iv) $\mathcal{R}$ is a function of $\mu$ and carries no information about any
outcome, so restricting both the calibration and the test cells to $\mathcal{R}$
gives coverage conditional on the selection event.
\end{IEEEproof}

One dependence deserves stating, because it is the place a careful reader will
push. $M$ is the mean of the same $\mu$ vector being deflated, so
$\hat{\lambda}_s$ is not a function of cell $s$ alone: every cell enters through
$M$. Two consequences. The marginal statement (ii) should be read conditional on
$M$, which costs nothing asymptotically --- $M$ concentrates at rate
$S^{-1/2}$, and $S = 4000$ in our simulation --- but is not exactly
finite-sample. And the selection rule in (iv) is likewise not a per-cell function
of covariates, which is what a textbook split-conformal argument would want.
Exchangeability survives regardless: $M$ is a symmetric function of the cells, so
permuting cells permutes $(\hat{\lambda}_s)$ and the retention indicators
together, leaving the joint law invariant. That is all conformal validity
requires. Estimating $M$ on the calibration split alone would remove the
dependence entirely at the cost of a noisier normalizer, and we flag it as the
cleaner choice for a deployment where exact finite-sample validity matters more
than efficiency.

Part (iv) is the honest content of the empirical result in
Section~\ref{sec:feedback}: coverage is restored \emph{on the cells the system is
willing to speak about}. We set $\kappa_{\mathrm{run}} = 0.9$ and $\bar{m} = 5$
following the implementation, and we report the retained fraction wherever
corrected coverage appears.

A caveat on identification, which we would rather state than let a reader assume
away. The difference-in-differences design identifies the recording elasticity
$\rho$; the loop gain is $\kappa = \beta\rho$, and the DiD carries an uncancelled
term in the unobserved latent level, so a separately assumed policy elasticity
$\beta$ is required. $\kappa$ is therefore \emph{not} point-identified by this
design alone, and on our real municipal data the DiD is a null. We validate the
correction where the truth is available --- in simulation --- and treat real-data
results as a sensitivity analysis at an assumed gain.

\section{Method}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Problem setup and ZINB likelihood}
Let $s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ index spatial units, $t \in \{1,\dots,T\}$ weeks, and
$c \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ crime categories (violent, property, drug, so $C = 3$).
Chicago has $S = 77$ community areas, New York $S = 78$ comparable units, and
both panels run $T = 313$ weeks. For every triple we predict a distribution over
counts rather than a number.

Crime counts are sparse and overdispersed at once, and the two need separate
machinery: sparsity is structural, since a cell can be zero because nothing
happened or because nothing was recorded, so a point mass at zero describes the
data rather than merely simplifying it. The model emits $(\pi,\mu,r)$ per cell
as in \eqref{eq:zinb},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zinb}
P(Y = y) =
\begin{cases}
\pi + (1-\pi)\, f_{\mathrm{NB}}(0), & y = 0,\\[2pt]
(1-\pi)\, f_{\mathrm{NB}}(y),       & y > 0,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where the negative binomial mass is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nb}
f_{\mathrm{NB}}(y) = \frac{\Gamma(y+r)}{\Gamma(r)\,y!}
  \left(\frac{r}{r+\mu}\right)^{\!r}
  \left(\frac{\mu}{r+\mu}\right)^{\!y}.
\end{equation}
Here $\pi \in (0,1)$ is the zero-inflation probability, the mass placed on a
structural zero; $\mu > 0$ is the mean of the negative binomial component; and
$r > 0$ is its dispersion parameter, with variance $\mu + \mu^2/r$, so small $r$
means heavy-tailed and $r \to \infty$ recovers a Poisson. The network produces
them as $\pi = \sigma(\cdot)$, $\mu = \mathrm{softplus}(\cdot)$ and
$r = \mathrm{softplus}(\cdot) + 0.1$. That floor on $r$ matters more than it
looks; see Section~\ref{sec:rcollapse}.

We reuse $\pi$ for the zero-inflation probability only. The allocation policy of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} is written $\varphi$ throughout to keep the two apart.

\subsection{Architecture}
Space first. Two adjacency structures feed a GATv2 encoder: queen contiguity for
immediate geographic spillover, and $k$-nearest neighbours in demographic feature
space for areas that behave alike without touching. Attention is computed
dynamically over the union of both neighbourhoods,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat}
\begin{aligned}
e_{ij} &= \mathbf{z}^{\!\top} \mathrm{LeakyReLU}
          \!\left(\mathbf{W}[\mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_j]\right),\\
\gamma_{ij} &= \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i}\exp(e_{ik})},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{N}_i = \mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{queen}}_i \cup
\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{knn}}_i$ in \eqref{eq:gat}, and node states update as the
attention-weighted sum over that union.

Then time. A spatiotemporal transformer over unified space--time tokens with a
structured attention mask carries temporal context. Between the two, a
multi-factor feature mixer with a Jensen--Shannon diversity penalty discourages
routing predictions through demographic covariates --- a partial guard against
proxy discrimination, not a solution to it.

We anchor $\mu$ to a trailing mean rather than predicting it free. This was not a
preference: without anchoring, conformal interval widths exceed 1000 and skill
score goes deeply negative. Anchoring holds the level and lets the network model
deviations from it.

\subsection{Training and the $r$-collapse problem}
\label{sec:rcollapse}
Training minimizes ZINB negative log-likelihood, computed with
$\mathrm{logsumexp}$ on the zero branch and $\log\Gamma$ elsewhere for stability.

One failure mode deserves description, because it is a clean example of a metric
lying. Under maximum likelihood, $r$ drifts toward its floor. As $r \to 0$ the
negative binomial variance $\mu + \mu^2/r$ implied by \eqref{eq:nb} diverges,
giving a heavy-tailed
distribution that assigns tolerable probability to almost any observation, so NLL
looks acceptable. MAE even improves, because the mode sharpens. But CRPS
degrades, because the predictive CDF is smeared. Likelihood and sharpness pull in
opposite directions and likelihood wins. The fix is the per-cell penalty
\eqref{eq:rreg},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rreg}
\mathcal{L}_{r\text{-reg}}
 = \lambda_r \cdot \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}
   \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \mathrm{ReLU}(r_{\mathrm{reg}} - r_i),
\end{equation}
with $r_{\mathrm{reg}} = 0.5$ and $\lambda_r = 0.1$. Per-cell rather than
batch-mean is the whole point: a batch-mean penalty lets some cells collapse to
near zero while others compensate by staying high, so the average looks fine
while individual predictive distributions are useless.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}
The model's own quantiles are not trustworthy, so we calibrate post hoc, on a
chronological split that never lets a later period inform an earlier one. Fix a
miscoverage level $\alpha$, so the target coverage is $1-\alpha$; we use
$\alpha = 0.1$ throughout. Let $q_p(x_i)$ denote the level-$p$ quantile of the
ZINB predictive distribution for a cell with covariates $x_i$, and let $n$ be the
number of calibration cells. Non-conformity uses the CQR score
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cqr}
V_i = \max\!\left(q_{\alpha/2}(x_i) - y_i,\; y_i - q_{1-\alpha/2}(x_i)\right),
\end{equation}
which is positive when $y_i$ falls outside the predictive interval and negative
when it falls inside, and the split-conformal threshold is the
$\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$ empirical quantile of
$\{V_1,\dots,V_n\}$. For per-group control over $G$ demographic groups, ECRC
computes a separate threshold per group with the Hoeffding slack
\eqref{eq:ecrc},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ecrc}
\epsilon_g = \sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta_g)}{2 n_g}},
\end{equation}
where $n_g$ is the number of calibration cells in group $g$ and $\delta_g$ is
that group's failure probability, Bonferroni-corrected as $\delta_g = \delta / G$
so the guarantee holds simultaneously across groups at level $\delta$. Group
coverage is then at least $1 - \alpha - \epsilon_g$ with probability
$1-\delta_g$. An adaptive temporal variant updates the per-group miscoverage
target online with a PID rule~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}; it gives a
long-run average guarantee, not a per-step one, and we report it as such.

We evaluate ten variants and select among them by constraint rather than by eye:
narrowest mean width subject to marginal coverage at or above the floor,
demographic disparity at or below 0.03, abstention at or below 1\%, and finite
metrics. The ceiling was fixed before we looked. Section~\ref{sec:conformal}
shows the constraint doing real work.

Two smaller decisions worth recording. Ensemble weights come from
entropy-regularized EMOS~\cite{gneiting2005calibrated} learned on calibration
data, falling back to uniform when the learned weights fail to beat uniform on an
internal holdout; the entropy term exists because unregularized EMOS placed
99.6\% of its mass on a single seed. And post-hoc distributional recalibration is
\emph{fitted but gated}: we compare recalibrated against original CRPS on an
internal calibration holdout, never on test, and apply the correction only if it
helps. On both cities it did not (Chicago $-5.29\%$, NYC $-0.66\%$), so both
report identity recalibration. Gating on test CRPS would have been leakage.

\subsection{Feedback-corrected latent intervals}
\label{sec:latent}
Everything in Section~\ref{sec:calibration} delivers coverage of $y$, the record.
Under \eqref{eq:fixedpoint} that is not coverage of $\lambda$, and the gap widens
with $\kappa$. Two worlds --- one with honest recording, one with a biased loop
--- can produce identical records, so no test on passive data separates
them~\cite{mendler2022anticipating}. This is the ``confidently wrong'' state:
coverage of the record holds while coverage of the truth silently fails.

Breaking the tie needs a perturbation. A staggered detection-sensitivity shock
shifts recording intensity for treated units, and a difference-in-differences on
log recorded rates estimates the recording response, subject to the
identification caveat in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. Given a gain estimate, we
deflate by \eqref{eq:deflation} and issue intervals on $\hat\lambda$.

Abstention is not optional. By Theorem~\ref{thm:deflation}(iii) the log-error
scales with $\log(\mu_s/M)$, which is unbounded, so we abstain when $\hat\kappa$
exceeds $\kappa_{\mathrm{run}} = 0.9$ or the required multiplier leaves
$[1/5, 5]$. The system says ``I do not know'' rather than issuing an interval it
cannot stand behind. That is the right behaviour, and it has a real cost that
Section~\ref{sec:feedback} measures rather than leaves implicit.

\section{Experimental Setup}
% =====================================================================
\textbf{Data.} Chicago (77 community areas) and New York (78 units), weekly
counts in three categories over 2018--2023, giving $T = 313$ weeks. The split is
strictly chronological, by week index: train $[0, 208)$ = 2018--2021, validation
$[208, 234)$ = 2022 H1 (early stopping and per-seed EMA-versus-raw selection),
calibration $[234, 260)$ = 2022 H2 (conformal thresholds and EMOS weights), test
$[260, 313)$ = 2023, the 53 weeks on which every number in this paper is
computed. Nothing from a later block informs an earlier one. Chicago test tensors
are $53 \times 77$. Demographic groups for the fairness audit come from
area-level composition and are fixed before evaluation.

\textbf{Baselines.} Six classical model families --- historical average, seasonal
naive, lag-1 persistence, STARIMA, plain ZINB and XGBoost --- with the historical
average evaluated in both a rolling and a frozen variant. Four deep
spatiotemporal models: LSTM-NB, TFT-ZINB, GraphWaveNet, STZINB-GNN. Deep
baselines run three seeds ($42, 137, 256$), reported as mean $\pm$ standard
deviation.

\textbf{Metrics.} CRPS is primary, being a strictly proper scoring rule for
distributional forecasts. We also report MAE, RMSE and Brier score on the zero
event. For calibration: marginal coverage, mean width, per-group and per-category
disparity, abstention rate, PIT uniformity $\chi^2$, and the Hersbach
decomposition~\cite{hersbach2000decomposition} of CRPS into reliability,
resolution and uncertainty.

\textbf{Significance.} Diebold--Mariano~\cite{diebold1995comparing} with
Newey--West adjustment on paired weekly losses, plus a stationary block
bootstrap. We treat a claim as supported only when both agree.

\textbf{Which baseline binds.} CRPSS against a \emph{frozen} historical average
looks impressive --- 0.2713 and 0.3165. We do not lead with it. A frozen baseline
is unrealistically weak, because a real deployment would update its average as
weeks arrive. Against a \emph{rolling} HA the gains are 0.0360 and 0.0494, and
the evaluation code marks \texttt{ha\_rolling} as binding. Those are the numbers
we quote.

The Hersbach decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty}, Fig.~\ref{fig:hersbach})

measured on all cells. \textbf{The two columns have different denominators.} This
is exactly what Theorem~\ref{thm:deflation}(iv) formalizes: the guarantee is
conditional on retention. The comparison still means something --- on the cells
where correction is trustworthy, coverage of the truth is restored from
catastrophic to near-nominal --- but anyone who reads $16\% \to 93\%$ as

fraction as a second panel and we label it wherever it appears. There is also a
cost at the other end: at $\kappa = 0$ the corrector abstains on 25\% of cells
while improving nothing ($0.950 \to 0.949$). The abstention rule is tuned for the
high-gain regime and overpays at low gain.

There is a check on Theorem~\ref{thm:amplification} hiding in this experiment,
and it passes. The theorem says amplification grows with the gain, so the
\emph{fraction} of disparity attributable to feedback --- and therefore the
fraction a correct deflation should remove --- ought to increase in $\kappa$ even
where the raw disparity does not. The relative reductions are $0\%$, $29.3\%$,
$47.1\%$, $67.1\%$ and $75.6\%$ across the grid: monotone in $\kappa$, including
across the $0.70 \to 0.85$ step where the absolute disparity falls. The
non-monotone absolute curve and the monotone relative curve are both what
\eqref{eq:powerlaw} predicts, which is a modest but genuine piece of evidence
that the amplification law describes the simulated mechanism rather than merely
labelling it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =====================================================================
We would rather list these ourselves.

\textbf{Chicago is misspecified.} The PIT histogram fails uniformity badly:
$\chi^2 = 241.84$, $p = 5.25\times10^{-47}$, maximum bin deviation 0.0368 against
an expected 0.10 per bin. The top bin holds 13.68\% of the mass and the rise
across upper bins is monotone, so the model systematically under-predicts
Chicago's right tail. NYC passes cleanly ($\chi^2 = 10.75$, $p = 0.293$), which
makes the Chicago failure a property of that city's data rather than a bug in
shared code. The spatial residuals say the same thing from another direction
(Section~\ref{sec:spatial}). Conformal coverage still holds at 90.75\%, because
split conformal's guarantee is marginal and finite-sample under exchangeability
and does not require a correctly specified model --- that is precisely why we
calibrate post hoc. But a correctly specified model would give tighter intervals,
and ours is not one.

\textbf{The correction's abstention cost is severe at high gain.} 85\% of cells
at $\kappa = 0.85$. A deployment there would decline to answer for most of the
city. That is honest behaviour and it is also a limited product.

\textbf{$\kappa$ is not point-identified.} The DiD gives the recording elasticity
$\rho$; the loop gain $\kappa = \beta\rho$ needs an assumed policy elasticity
$\beta$, and the DiD carries an uncancelled latent-level term. Real-data results
are sensitivity analyses at assumed $\kappa$. On real data our DiD is a null.

\textbf{The architecture does not win on its own.} Single-seed CRPS around 3.3622
loses to TFT-ZINB's 2.9456 on Chicago. Five-seed EMOS is doing the work.

\textbf{OICC trades one fairness metric for another.} Best hit rate and best
over-allocation ratio, worst allocation disparity; at small budgets it is worst
on hit rate as well.

\textbf{Skill over a realistic baseline is modest.} 3.6\% and 4.9\% CRPSS against
the rolling HA. The 27--32\% figures are against a frozen baseline that no
deployment would use.

\textbf{Fairness here is coverage-based.} Equal coverage across groups is one
notion among many, computed on area-level demographic composition rather than
individual attributes, and it says nothing about whether the underlying
allocation is just.

\textbf{The simulation is a simulation.} The coverage-restoration result assumes
our own generative model of the feedback loop. It is the only setting where latent
coverage is measurable, which is the point, but it is not field evidence.

\textbf{Five seeds is fewer than we would like.} Our configuration notes that
seven to eight seeds would be needed for paired $t$-tests at power 0.80 with
$d = 0.8$.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
Records are not reality, and a forecaster validated only against records cannot
tell the difference. We built a spatiotemporal ZINB forecaster that is
competitive on ordinary terms --- CRPS 2.8267 and 3.1401, eight of eight
Diebold--Mariano wins over deep baselines at $p < 2\times10^{-6}$, conformal coverage of
90.75\% and 90.02\% under a pre-registered 3\% disparity ceiling --- and then
asked the question those terms cannot answer: is the model right about crime, or
only about crime records?

In simulation the answer is stark. At feedback gain 0.85, intervals that look
well calibrated against the record cover the latent process 16.2\% of the time.
Deflating the record by the estimated feedback multiplier restores that to
93.0\%, on the 15\% of cells where the correction is trustworthy enough to issue
an interval at all. On real records from two cities, the same correction cuts
exposure disparity for higher-minority areas by 58\% and 61\% under an assumed
gain.

What we would want a reader to take away is narrower than the numbers might
suggest. The correction works, in a regime we can measure, at a cost we can
quantify. It abstains heavily when the feedback gain approaches runaway, and its
gain parameter is not point-identified on real data. Chicago's PIT failure says
our own predictor is misspecified on one of the two cities we tested.

The obvious next step is the natural experiment: run the detection-shock DiD on a
real staggered rollout with enough power to move the estimate off null. Until
then, the honest claim is that we have shown a correctable failure is correctable
in a setting where correctness is checkable --- and that the abstention rate, not
the coverage number, is the thing to watch.

\section*{Reproducibility}
% =====================================================================
Every number reported here is either read from a result file under
\texttt{outputs/} or reproduced by running a script under \texttt{scripts/}. The
three feedback-correction figures are generated by
\texttt{scripts/make\_paper\_figures.py}, which calls the experiment functions
directly and writes the values it plotted to
\texttt{outputs/figure\_data/*.json}, so any figure can be checked against the
numbers behind it. Tables~\ref{tab:main_results}--\ref{tab:policy-simulation} are
generated by \texttt{scripts/ablation\_study.py} and converted to this
manuscript's floats by \texttt{scripts/build\_paper\_tables.py}; neither is
hand-edited. The spatial residual map is computed from the saved test predictions
in \texttt{outputs/conformal\_evaluation/chicago\_predictions.npz}.

Three figures that previously existed in our repository are deliberately absent.
Two attention visualizations drew synthetic weights --- an exponential distance
decay for the spatial map, a hand-built lag pattern for the temporal one --- under
titles claiming they showed learned GATv2 and transformer attention. Our pipeline
does not persist real attention weights, so those figures could not be produced
honestly, and the plotting functions now refuse to run without real input rather
than drawing something misleading.

Code and generated artifacts accompany the manuscript. The scripts named above
regenerate every table and figure from the stored evaluation outputs without
retraining, and the feedback-correction experiments are seeded, so
Table~\ref{tab:feedback} and Figs.~\ref{fig:latent}--\ref{fig:crosscity}
reproduce exactly.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
% =====================================================================
A paper about forecasting crime should say what it is not claiming. We do not
argue that better forecasts make predictive policing appropriate, and correcting
a biased record does not make the record's provenance acceptable. What the
correction does is narrower and, we think, more useful: it makes a specific
failure \emph{visible and measurable}, namely that a model can pass every
available validation check while its intervals have stopped covering reality in
exactly the neighbourhoods that were over-policed to begin with. A system that
cannot detect that failure should not be deployed; one that can, at least
surfaces it for the people affected.

Three consequences we would want honoured by anyone building on this. The
abstention rate, not the coverage number, is the operational figure of merit: at
$\kappa = 0.85$ our corrector declines to answer for $85\%$ of cells, and a
deployment in that regime is a deployment that mostly says ``I do not know'',
which is the correct output and also a reason not to ship it. The fairness notion
we audit is equal \emph{coverage} across groups computed from area-level
composition, which is one criterion among many and says nothing about whether the
allocation it informs is just --- Section~\ref{sec:limitations} shows two
reasonable fairness metrics disagreeing about the same policy. And the routing
and allocation results are simulations of decision rules, not field trials; we
report them as evidence about the rules, not about outcomes for real people.
