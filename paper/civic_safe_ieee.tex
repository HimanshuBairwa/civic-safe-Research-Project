\title{Feedback-Corrected Conformal Prediction for Spatiotemporal Crime Forecasting}

\author{Himanshu~Bairwa%
\thanks{H. Bairwa is with the Department of Computer Science. Corresponding author email: himanshubairwa@example.com.}%

\begin{abstract}
Crime records are not crime. Police records are produced by a loop: past records direct patrols, patrols produce records, and the loop amplifies whatever disparity it started with. A forecaster fit on such records can be well calibrated against the record and badly wrong about reality, and no amount of held-out validation on records will reveal it. We build CIVIC-SAFE, a spatiotemporal forecaster that predicts a full zero-inflated negative binomial distribution of weekly crime counts per community area, and we pair it with a correction for this measurement problem. Two contributions. First, the applied system: a dual-graph GATv2 spatiotemporal transformer with a five-seed entropy-regularized EMOS ensemble reaches CRPS 2.8267 on Chicago and 3.1401 on New York for 2023, beating four deep baselines in all eight head-to-head Diebold-Mariano tests at $p < 10^{-6}$, with conformal intervals achieving 90.75\% and 90.02\% coverage under a pre-registered 3\% disparity ceiling. Second, and the reason for the paper: we deflate the recorded rate by a feedback multiplier and issue intervals for the latent process. In simulation at feedback gain 0.85, intervals calibrated on the record retain only 16.2\% coverage of true incidence while our corrected intervals hold 93.0\%---though they abstain on 85\% of cells to do it, a cost we quantify rather than hide. On real records from both cities, correction cuts exposure disparity by 58\% and 61\%. We report what fails too: Chicago's probability integral transform is non-uniform at $p = 5.25 \times 10^{-47}$, and 65 of its 77 areas come in low.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{S}{tart} with the thing that makes this problem different from ordinary forecasting. When a weather model predicts rain, the prediction does not change whether it rains. When a crime model predicts crime, the prediction sends officers, and officers find crime. Not because they manufacture it, but because crime that nobody looks for goes unrecorded. Send more patrols to a block and the recorded rate there rises even if the underlying rate held perfectly still.

So the training data is an artifact of past decisions by the system you are trying to improve.

This has an uncomfortable consequence that we think has been under-appreciated. Take a forecaster and validate it the normal way---hold out 2023, compute Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS), check the coverage of your prediction intervals. Suppose everything passes. You have learned that the model predicts \emph{records} well. You have learned nothing about whether it predicts \emph{crime} well, and the two come apart exactly where it matters most: in the neighborhoods that were over-policed to begin with. Ensign et al.~\cite{ensign2018runaway} named this loop and showed it runs away. van Amsterdam et al.~\cite{vanamsterdam2025when} showed the general pattern of a model that is accurate and harmful at once, invisible to passive validation. What nobody has offered is a constructive fix.

That is what we build here.

Our starting point is a fixed-point model. Latent incidence $\lambda_s$ in area $s$ is unobserved. A policy allocates attention $a_s = \pi(\mu_s)$ based on the recorded-rate estimate $\mu_s$. Recording is attention-dependent: $y_s \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_s g(a_s))$ with $g$ strictly increasing. A learner that fits the records converges to the self-consistent equilibrium:
\begin{equation}
\mu_s = \lambda_s g(\pi(\mu_s)).
\end{equation}
Define the feedback gain $\kappa$ as:
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \left(\frac{d \log a}{d \log \mu}\right) \left(\frac{d \log g}{d \log a}\right),
\end{equation}
the product of how strongly allocation responds to the record and how strongly recording responds to allocation. From this closed-loop structure, we obtain:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d \log \mu_s}{d \log \lambda_s} = \frac{1}{1 - \kappa},
\end{equation}
so recorded disparity is true disparity raised to the power of $1/(1 - \kappa)$, with a singular pole at $\kappa = 1$. We want to be careful about credit here, because this closed form is classic. It is the closed-loop gain of a feedback amplifier, and in economics it is the social multiplier of Glaeser, Sacerdote and Scheinkman~\cite{glaeser2003social}. We claim neither. What we contribute is the constructive step that follows: given an estimate of $\kappa$, deflate the record by the recording multiplier and issue prediction intervals valid for $\lambda$, not for $y$, with principled abstention where the correction becomes untrustworthy.

The empirical picture, in brief. CIVIC-SAFE forecasts a full Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) distribution per area-week-category and achieves CRPS 2.8267 (Chicago) and 3.1401 (NYC) on 2023, ahead of every baseline we tried, with all eight head-to-head Diebold-Mariano comparisons against deep baselines significant at $p < 10^{-6}$. Post-hoc conformal calibration lands at 90.75\% and 90.02\% coverage against a 90\% target while keeping demographic coverage disparity at 0.0238 and 0.0286, both under a ceiling we fixed at 0.03 before looking. Then the correction: in a closed-loop simulation at $\kappa = 0.85$, intervals calibrated on the record cover the latent process only 16.2\% of the time, while corrected intervals hold 93.0\%. On real Chicago and NYC records, correction reduces recorded-exposure disparity for the higher-minority stratum by 58\% and 61\%.

We also report what fails. Chicago's PIT histogram is non-uniform ($\chi^2 = 241.84$ on nine degrees of freedom, $p = 5.25 \times 10^{-47}$)---the ZINB is misspecified in Chicago's right tail. A single CIVIC-SAFE seed loses to TFT-ZINB; our advantage arises from 5-seed EMOS ensembling. The corrected intervals at $\kappa = 0.85$ abstain on 85\% of cells, so the headline 16\% $\to$ 93\% compares two different denominators. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} details these aspects.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A feedback-corrected conformal predictor that restores coverage of the unobserved latent process from a biased record, equipped with a safety abstention tripwire (Section~\ref{sec:latent_correction}).
    \item A coordinate-free elasticity formulation of policing feedback loops, establishing the amplification power law with explicit singularity at $\kappa = 1$.
    \item An applied probabilistic forecaster with finite-sample conformal validity, comparing nine calibration variants under a pre-registered demographic fairness ceiling.
    \item A full forensic accounting of failure modes, identifying spatial residual concentration and count misspecification in real municipal data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Spatiotemporal count forecasting.} ZINB output heads on graph networks are established for sparse urban counts. STZINB-GNN~\cite{zhuang2022uncertainty} pairs a zero-inflated negative binomial likelihood with a spatiotemporal graph encoder for demand prediction, and STMGNN-ZINB~\cite{wang2024spatiotemporal} extends this with multi-graph fusion. Our predictor sits in this family. GATv2~\cite{brody2022attentive} supplies our spatial attention operator across queen contiguity and $k$-nearest demographic neighbours.

\textbf{Conformal prediction.} Split conformal prediction provides distribution-free finite-sample coverage under exchangeability. Conformalized quantile regression (CQR)~\cite{romano2019conformalized} supplies the conformity score. Adaptive conformal inference~\cite{gibbs2021adaptive} handles temporal drift, and equalized conditional risk control (ECRC)~\cite{feldman2021improving} enforces group-conditional coverage. However, standard conformal prediction guarantees coverage exclusively for the observed variable $y$ (the record), creating a blind spot when records diverge from truth.

\textbf{Feedback loops in predictive policing.} Ensign et al.~\cite{ensign2018runaway} formalized runaway policing feedback via Pólya-urn dynamics, demonstrating convergence to degenerate allocation. Hashimoto et al.~\cite{hashimoto2018fairness} and Wyllie et al.~\cite{wyllie2024fairness} analyzed disparity amplification under repeated retraining. We sharpen this by deriving a closed-form amplification elasticity with a finite pole at $\kappa = 1$, and by providing an actionable corrective estimator.

\textbf{Performativity and identification.} Performative prediction~\cite{perdomo2020performative,perdomo2025revisiting} formalizes distributions that shift in response to deployed models. Performative risk control~\cite{li2025performative} bounds risk on observed outcomes; in contrast, our challenge is bounding coverage on an \emph{unobserved latent variable} $\lambda$. Perturbation-based identification templates~\cite{mendler2022anticipating} motivate our use of detection shocks.

\textbf{Natural experiments in criminology.} Staggered acoustic gunshot detection deployments (e.g., ShotSpotter) have been evaluated as quasi-experiments~\cite{connealy2024staggered}. While criminologists estimate reduced-form policy impacts, we repurpose the staggered difference-in-differences design to estimate the structural recording elasticity.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Problem Setup and ZINB Likelihood}
Let $s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ index spatial units, $t \in \{1,\dots,T\}$ index weeks, and $c \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ index crime categories (violent, property, drug). We predict a full predictive distribution over counts. Chicago contains $S=77$ community areas; New York City contains $S=78$ police precinct aggregates.

Crime counts exhibit extreme structural sparsity and heavy overdispersion simultaneously. We model counts $y_{s,t,c}$ using a Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial likelihood:
\begin{align}
P(Y = y) = 
\begin{cases}
\pi + (1 - \pi) \left(\frac{r}{r + \mu}\right)^r, & y = 0, \\
(1 - \pi) \frac{\Gamma(y+r)}{\Gamma(r) y!} \left(\frac{r}{r + \mu}\right)^r \left(\frac{\mu}{r + \mu}\right)^y, & y > 0,
\end{cases}
\end{align}
where $\pi = \sigma(\cdot) \in (0,1)$ is the zero-inflation probability, $\mu = \text{softplus}(\cdot) > 0$ is the negative binomial mean, and $r = \text{softplus}(\cdot) + 0.1$ is the dispersion parameter.

\subsection{Architecture}
The spatial encoder deploys GATv2 attention across dual graphs:
\begin{equation}
e_{ij} = \mathbf{a}^T \text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{W} [\mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_j]\right), \quad \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp(e_{ik})},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_i$ is the union of queen geographic neighbours and demographic $k$-nearest neighbours. A spatiotemporal transformer with causal masking captures historical dependencies. To prevent demographic shortcut learning, a multi-factor feature mixer regularized by Jensen-Shannon divergence constrains covariate representations. Crucially, $\mu$ is parameterized as an offset anchored to a trailing historical mean; unanchored training causes conformal interval widths to explode beyond 1,000.

\subsection{Training and the $r$-Collapse Pathology}
Training directly with maximum likelihood causes a severe degeneration: $r$ collapses toward its lower bound. As $r \to 0$, the negative binomial variance $\mu + \mu^2/r \to \infty$. The resulting diffuse distribution achieves an acceptable NLL across outliers, but predictive sharpness degrades and CRPS deteriorates. To enforce sharpness, we inject a per-cell penalty:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{r\text{-reg}} = \lambda_r \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \text{ReLU}(r_{\text{reg}} - r_i),
\end{equation}
with $r_{\text{reg}} = 0.5$ and $\lambda_r = 0.1$. Penalizing individual cells rather than the batch average is essential to prevent heterogeneous collapse.

\subsection{Conformal Calibration and Selection Policy}
To guarantee rigorous finite-sample coverage, we partition data chronologically: training (2018--2021), validation (2022-H1), calibration (2022-H2), and test (2023). Non-conformity scores follow Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR):
\begin{equation}
s_i = \max\left(q_{\alpha/2}(x_i) - y_i, \, y_i - q_{1-\alpha/2}(x_i)\right).
\end{equation}
For demographic fairness, Equalized Conditional Risk Control (ECRC) computes group-specific thresholds with Hoeffding bounds:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_g = \sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta_g)}{2 n_g}}.
\end{equation}
An adaptive temporal PID calibrator~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal} dynamically tracks drift.

We evaluate nine conformal variants. The production calibrator is selected via a pre-registered optimization rule: minimize interval width subject to marginal coverage $\ge 90\%$, demographic disparity $\Delta_{\text{dem}} \le 0.03$, and zero invalid metrics.

\subsection{Feedback-Corrected Latent Prediction Intervals}
\label{sec:latent_correction}
When policing decisions depend on predictions, records reflect deployment rather than crime. Staggered detection shocks identify the recording elasticity $\rho$ via difference-in-differences. Given the loop gain $\kappa = \beta \rho$, we compute the recording multiplier:
\begin{equation}
m_s = \left(\frac{\mu_s}{M}\right)^\kappa, \quad \hat{\lambda}_s = \frac{\mu_s}{m_s},
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the city-wide baseline mean. Conformal intervals are then deflated to cover the latent parameter $\hat{\lambda}_s$.

To prevent variance explosion as $\kappa \to 1$, we introduce a safety abstention tripwire: the model abstains from issuing intervals whenever $\kappa \ge 0.9$ or $m_s \notin [0.2, 5.0]$.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Datasets.} Chicago (77 community areas, 2018--2023) and New York City (78 precinct clusters, 2018--2023) aggregated weekly across violent, property, and drug crimes. The test set comprises 53 weeks of 2023 evaluated in an out-of-sample rolling-window fashion.

\textbf{Baselines.} Six classical models (rolling Historical Average, frozen Historical Average, Seasonal Naive, Lag-1 Persistence, STARIMA, XGBoost) and four deep spatiotemporal models (LSTM-NB, TFT-ZINB, GraphWaveNet, STZINB-GNN). Deep baselines are evaluated across three seeds ($42, 137, 256$).

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.} Primary metric: Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS). Statistical significance is assessed via the Diebold-Mariano test with Newey-West HAC covariance ($h = \lfloor T^{1/3} \rfloor = 3$) and 10,000-resample stationary block bootstrapping. Calibration is evaluated via marginal coverage, interval width, demographic coverage disparity, and Hersbach decomposition~\cite{hersbach2000decomposition}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chicago Tail Misspecification:} Chicago fails PIT uniformity ($\chi^2 = 241.84, p = 5.25 \times 10^{-47}$), underpredicting extreme tail events on the West Side. NYC passes ($\chi^2 = 10.75, p = 0.293$). Conformal prediction guarantees marginal validity despite this misspecification.
    \item \textbf{Abstention Overhead:} At $\kappa = 0.85$, the corrector abstains on $85\%$ of cells to maintain $93\%$ coverage.
    \item \textbf{Identification Limits:} On passive real-world data, the DiD is statistically null; real-city results represent sensitivity analyses under assumed $\kappa$.
    \item \textbf{Single-Seed Performance:} A single seed (CRPS 3.3622) is outperformed by TFT-ZINB (2.9456); our margin requires 5-seed EMOS ensembling.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
Records are an artifact of historical policing allocation. We demonstrated that standard conformal intervals fail to cover latent crime under performative feedback, collapsing to 16.2\% coverage. Our feedback-corrected conformal estimator restores latent coverage to 93.0\%, providing a mathematically certified path toward uncorrupted municipal prediction.
